The Snow Leopard Returns: Silence and Comeback of the King of the Plateau

2026.08.15 Roger Liu · Wild Note

The photo arrived the way the best news usually does: quietly. A camera trap on a scree slope in Sanjiangyuan fired at three in the morning and caught a blurred constellation of spots sliding through the dark. For the rangers who checked the memory card that morning, it was proof of something they had only dared to hope — the snow leopard was back on a ridge where it had not been photographed in years.

Snow leopards are built to be unseen. Their coat is the exact grey of granite lichen; their thick tail works like a balancing rope on cliff edges; their paws spread wide as snowshoes. They live where people rarely choose to live — three to five thousand meters up, in thin air and broken rock. But invisible is not the same as untouched. For decades the mountains went quiet for a reason, and the reason was us.

Why the mountains went quiet

In the 1980s and 1990s, poaching for pelts and bones pushed snow leopard numbers down sharply. Then came slower pressures: blue sheep, their main prey, declining as domestic herds competed for the same grass; a warming climate nudging the treeline upward and squeezing the habitat into ever thinner ribbons. A species can vanish from a valley without a single gunshot being heard nearby. It disappears by subtraction — a little less prey, a little less cover, a litter fewer each year.

What changed in recent years is not a fence. It is an economy. Herders are now paid as rangers and monitors; families that lose livestock to leopards can claim compensation instead of retaliation; camera traps have turned local people into the scientists of their own mountains. Conservation, I have come to believe, is less about keeping animals in and more about making room in the human ledger for them to stay.

A snow leopard survives on a mountain only when the people below it can afford to let it live there.

What a student can do

I will probably never survey the valleys of Sanjiangyuan. But the method — patient evidence, community economics, coexistence by design — scales down to any landscape. My own small versions are a bird-window collision survey around campus, roadkill records along a few familiar roads, and learning to read a habitat map the way I read a textbook. Attention, it turns out, is the entry fee to conservation.

The blurred spots on that photograph are more than an animal. They are a question handed to my generation: will our mountains be quiet because they are empty, or quiet because they are full — and safe? The return of the king of the plateau suggests the second answer is still possible. Keeping it possible is the work ahead.

— Roger Liu
