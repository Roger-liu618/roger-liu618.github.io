The Silence of Wetlands: A History of Species Loss in a Reed Marsh

2026.08.02 Roger Liu · Wild Note

As a child, the wetland near my hometown was a wall of sound. Frogs started before the rain even arrived, a rolling chorus that swelled and ebbed all night; herons argued at first light; in the reeds, something rustled constantly — warblers, moorhens, things I never learned to name. I thought that was simply what a wetland was: loud, crowded, indifferent to us.

This spring I went back with a notebook. The water is still there. The reeds are still there. What is missing is everything that used to live between them. I stood for an hour at dusk and heard — almost nothing. It was not the peaceful silence of wild places. It was a subtracted silence, the sound of absent things.

An anatomy of disappearance

Talking with neighbors, I pieced together the sequence. Sections of reed were cleared for a road and a storage yard; the water table was drawn down as pumps served new construction; fertilizer and pesticide runoff threaded in from upstream fields. Amphibians need shallow, fish-free water to breed; herons need those frogs and small fish in return. Each change looked minor on its own. Stacked together, they rebuilt the wetland into a place that no longer works for the species that made it loud.

What unsettles me most is not the loss itself but how quietly it was accepted. My younger cousin, who grew up after the change, thinks the marsh was always this quiet. Ecologists call this the shifting baseline: every generation inherits a smaller version of nature and mistakes it for the original. The history of a reed marsh, then, is not written in dramatic collapses. It is a ledger of small absences, each entry easy to miss.

You don't notice the hole in the world until you remember what used to live in it.

Learning to listen

There is a discipline for hearing what is missing: acoustic monitoring. Volunteers record frog calls with ordinary phones at fixed points and fixed times; software turns the recordings into spectrograms and call-activity indices. The data is simple, cheap, and honest — a census taken by ear. I joined a small survey like this, and what struck me was not the technology but the posture: counting is a form of caring. You cannot grieve for, or defend, a species you never learned to notice.

Silence, it turns out, is data too. If we keep records — of what sang here, and when, and in what numbers — then even silence can testify. The reed marsh near my hometown may never fully regain its chorus. But the next wetland over still can, if enough people remember what a wetland is supposed to sound like.

— Roger Liu
